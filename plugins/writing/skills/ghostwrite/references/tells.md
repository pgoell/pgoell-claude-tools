# Tell check

What makes a draft read as model-written, in the order to look for it. Flag what you find; do not fix it. The owner decides.

## Why the order matters

The strongest tells are not words. A model fills gaps: it supplies causes, motives, certainty, and tidy endings the owner never gave. A draft can pass a banned-word scan and still read as generated because of that. So check the draft against its sources first, and look at words last.

If you have the sources (interview notes, owner documents, the repository), run passes 1 to 3 against them. If you have only finished prose, phrase passes 1 to 3 as questions ("Did the owner say this caused that?"), never as findings. Passes 4 to 7 need no sources.

## Pass 1: Added substance

Anything that makes the piece hang together but traces to no source:

- motives, feelings, or thoughts the owner did not report;
- times, frequencies, sequences ("for weeks", "every time", "at first");
- a cause linking two events the owner only listed side by side;
- outcomes, follow-up changes, or results nobody recorded.

Test: point at the source line. If you cannot, flag it. Sounding plausible is not a source.

## Pass 2: Raised certainty

The draft states something more firmly than the owner did:

- "I suspect" became "the problem was";
- one test became a general finding;
- an experiment still running became a lesson learned;
- an idea that helped became proof.

Test: compare each claim with the owner's own wording. Flag any claim that got stronger. Real uncertainty the owner stated stays; flag only hedges with no source behind them (pass 6).

## Pass 3: Forced closure

The draft settles what the owner left open:

- every example supports the thesis, and none pulls against it;
- one cause explains everything;
- the ending resolves a tension the owner did not resolve;
- events told as a sequence become a deliberate plan.

Test: did the owner mention a doubt, an exception, or an open question that the draft dropped? Flag the drop.

## Pass 4: The piece talking about itself

Sentences that comment on the argument instead of making it. These use plain words, so word lists miss them.

- Rating its own evidence: "the most telling result", "here is the part that convinced me".
- Signposting: "which brings us to", "the second half of the story", "there is a catch".
- Labelling status: "the open question is", "one finding complicates this".
- Framing a quote before giving it: "she said it best".

Tests: delete the sentence; if the argument survives and only guidance is lost, flag it. Or look for nouns that point at the piece rather than the subject: the story, the picture, the case, the takeaway.

Keep: a sentence that decides between two findings ("the log rules out the network") is a claim, not commentary.

## Pass 5: Performed shape

Structure and voice the material did not call for:

- sections of equal length with the same internal pattern;
- lists padded or cut to three items;
- a short punchline closing most paragraphs;
- several jokes, coinages, or asides in one paragraph, or a voice-note habit used more often than the owner uses it;
- one metaphor carried through headings and transitions after it stopped explaining anything;
- a line that could move to an unrelated piece by swapping its nouns ("speed without direction is just motion").

Test for density, not presence. One aside in a paragraph can be the owner; four is a performance.

## Pass 6: Stock phrasing

- Scene-setting openers: "In today's world", "With the rise of", "Let's dive in", "It's worth noting".
- Inflated words where a plain word or a number would do: pivotal, crucial, delve, leverage, utilize, robust, seamless, transformative, foster, underscore.
- Business abstraction hiding a plain claim: stakeholders, pain points, actionable insights, alignment, value proposition. Name the person, problem, or decision instead.
- Hedges with no source behind them: "arguably", "to some extent", "it depends".
- Faceless authority: "studies show", "experts agree", with no named source.
- "Not X but Y" contrasts, more than one per piece.
- Summary closers: "In conclusion", "Ultimately", a last paragraph that repeats the piece.
- Em-dashes, en-dashes, middle-dot separators.

Keep a precise domain term even if it appears above (a "robust estimator" is statistics, not puffery).

## Pass 7: The owner's own list

Anything under Avoid in the voice note, and any tell the owner has asked you to watch for.

## Reporting

List findings in draft order: the quoted line, the pass it falls under, and one sentence on what is missing or added. No severity labels, no scores. Report at most ten; if there are more, say how many and show the ten that carry the most weight, passes 1 to 3 first.

No single finding proves the piece reads as generated. When the owner confirms a tell they want caught every time, add it to the voice note's Avoid list.
