# Interview and dictation

How to pull the substance of a piece out of the owner before any prose exists. Runs in the main thread, because subagents cannot ask the user questions.

## The authorship boundary

The owner owns the claim, the stakes, and the stories. You own the questions and, later, the sentences.

- Ask one question at a time and wait for the answer. Build the next question on what they said.
- Do not suggest answers, examples, or structure. "Was it something like X?" plants X.
- Do not upgrade a tentative remark. If the owner says "I think it might have been the cache", the draft says that, not "the cache caused it".
- Do not invent connective tissue. If the owner gave you A and C, ask how they get from A to C instead of writing B yourself.
- Note where the owner struggles to answer. That usually means they have not settled the point yet; say so rather than papering over it.
- Stop when you have enough, or when the owner says so. A short piece needs three or four answers, not twelve.

## Dictation

Offer it as an alternative to question and answer: "Would you rather just talk it through? Tell me the piece as if you were explaining it to a colleague, and I will capture it." Then:

1. Let them talk. Do not interrupt with structure.
2. Play back a short list of the points you heard, in their words, and ask what is missing or wrong.
3. Ask follow-ups only for gaps the draft cannot bridge without inventing something.

Dictated text carries the owner's phrasing. Keep their words where you can.

## Question bank by genre

Pick from these; do not ask them all. Ask in roughly this order, and skip anything the owner has already answered.

**Blog post, essay, talk script**

1. Why this, why now? What set it off?
2. Who is it for, and what do they already believe?
3. What have you seen, shipped, tried, or got wrong that anchors it? One concrete case.
4. What is the smartest version of "you are wrong", and what do you say back?
5. What are you leaving out, even though it is tempting?
6. What is the one sentence the reader should remember? (the throughline, Step 3)

**Client memo, briefing, decision doc**

1. Who reads it, and what decision or action do you want from them?
2. What is your recommendation, in one sentence?
3. Why is it right? Give me the reasons, and which document or number backs each one.
4. What does the reader believe now that this has to move?
5. What are the risks or objections, and how do you answer them?

**Email**

1. What should the reader do after reading it, and by when?
2. What do they need to know to do it?

**Pull request description**

1. Why was this change made? (The diff shows what changed; only the owner knows why.)

**README, how-to, design doc**

Most facts come from the repository, not the owner. Ask only:

1. Who is the reader, and what do they need to do?
2. Why was it built this way? (Only if the doc explains a design choice and no existing doc records the reason.)

## The throughline gate

The owner writes the one sentence the reader should remember. Keep it verbatim: it anchors the outline and the draft.

- If it is a topic ("our onboarding process") rather than a claim, ask for the claim: "What about onboarding should the reader believe after reading?"
- If it hedges both ways ("it depends"), ask which way it leans for this reader.
- Ask at most twice, then accept what the owner gives you. The gate exists to make the owner decide, not to wear them down.

## Recording the answers

Keep a short running summary of what the owner said, in their words, grouped by what the draft will need: throughline, reader, claims with their sources, stories, counterarguments, cuts. Show it back before the outline so the owner can correct it. This summary is the only fact base for the draft.
