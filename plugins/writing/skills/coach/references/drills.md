# Drills

The coach's curriculum. Five drills in a fixed order, higher-order concerns first, each built on a principle with a reason behind it. Work on the owner's real text wherever possible; an exercise is the fallback.

## Moving through the drills

- Start at drill 1 unless the coaching log shows the owner already passes it.
- **Advance** when the logged fault for the current drill stops recurring across two or three pieces.
- **Return** to an earlier drill when its fault comes back in the log. Say so ("the point slipped again in this memo, so back to drill 1 for this piece").
- Record the current drill in the coaching log.

## Drill 1: Point first

Principle: value lies in what the reader gets, not in the writer's effort. A piece needs one point that changes what its reader thinks or does.

- Ask the owner to state the piece's point in one sentence, without looking at the draft.
- Compare it with what the draft says first. If the point sits in the last paragraph, or nowhere, that is the fault.
- Test: would the reader say "so what?" to the sentence? Who, specifically, would disagree with it before reading?
- Fault to log: "no point", "point buried", "point is a topic".

## Drill 2: Structure

Principle: answer first, then support grouped so each group answers the question the answer raises. A reader holds a structure only when it is visible.

- Run the Socratic structure drill in `../ghostwrite/references/pyramid-audits.md` (the path is relative to the coach skill's directory; the file is shared with the ghostwrite skill): the owner writes the question, the answer, the plural noun, each point, and each piece of evidence. You ask and check; you never offer candidates.
- Then have the owner compare their draft's order with the pyramid they just built and reorder the draft themselves.
- Use the four audits there as the bank of structure issues.
- Fault to log: the audit and failure name (overlap, gap, label instead of finding, mixed group).

## Drill 3: Clear sentences

Principle (Williams): readers find a sentence clear when its main characters are the subjects and its key actions are the verbs.

- Diagnose: have the owner underline the first seven or eight words of each sentence in a paragraph. If the subject is not a character (a person, a team, a system) or the verb is not the action, the sentence is a candidate.
- Common faults: actions hidden in nouns ("make a decision" for "decide", "the implementation of" for "implement"), empty subjects ("There is", "It is important that"), long introductory phrases before the subject.
- The owner revises each candidate. You show one as the model.
- Fault to log: "nominalization", "empty subject", "late subject".

## Drill 4: Cohesion

Principle (Williams): readers follow a passage when each sentence starts with information they already have and ends with the new, important part (the stress position).

- Diagnose: look at the start of each sentence in a paragraph. Does it pick up something from the sentence before? Look at the end: is the important word there, or a trailing qualifier?
- The owner reorders the sentences or the parts inside them.
- Fault to log: "new before old", "weak stress position".

## Drill 5: Cutting

Principle: remove what the reader does not need. Needless exposition is the edit expert editors make most often after word choice and sentence structure. The writer cannot see it right away because they already know what they meant (the curse of knowledge).

- Have the owner cut the draft by a set share (for example a fifth) without losing a point, then read it after time away or give it to a colleague.
- Targets: throat-clearing openers, restating what was just said, background the reader already has, hedges stacked on hedges.
- Fault to log: "throat-clearing", "restates", "over-explains".

## Optional drills between pieces

Offer these when the owner wants practice without a live piece. Short and regular beats long and rare; do not make them a daily requirement.

- **Franklin reconstruction.** The owner picks a short piece they admire, writes one-line hint notes per paragraph, puts the original away for a day, rewrites it from the notes, then compares. The coach asks what the original does that theirs does not; the owner names it.
- **Scales.** Pick one dimension (point, structure, sentence clarity, cohesion). Show two or three short samples, the owner's included; the owner rates each on that one dimension and says why, then revises the weakest one of their own.
- **Sentence combining.** Give the owner three to five short, choppy sentences drawn from their own draft; they combine them into one or two in several different ways and pick the version that best puts the important point last. The coach says what each version emphasizes, without writing one.
